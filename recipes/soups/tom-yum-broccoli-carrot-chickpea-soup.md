# Tom Yum Broccoli, Carrot & Chickpea Soup

*The first Singapore cook. A chunky tom yum soup with **two coconut-and-soy liquids and no stock** — a whole 270 ml can of 🥥 Ayam Pure Coconut **Cream** (28.5% fat, no gums) for richness and a litre of 🥛 F&N NutriSoy for volume, protein and an unusually large calcium load. 🫘 Chickpeas go in from dry in the pressure cooker, 🥕 carrots simmer in the broth, 🥦 broccoli is steamed apart, and the soup is ladled over a base of 🌾 buckwheat cooked in the same pot the chickpeas came out of. **Three of the five components are finished outside the soup** and meet it in the bowl. ⚠ Two hazards govern the whole method: **soy protein curdles in acid**, and **acid turns broccoli olive-drab** — both are solved by keeping things out of the pot rather than by cooking them differently. No air fryer here, so the 🥦 broccoli's bite is the only textural contrast; don't overshoot it. Serves 5.*

## Method

> ⚠ **Two rules govern this recipe, and both are about keeping things *out* of the pot.**
> **(1) Soy curdles in acid.** Soy protein flocculates around pH 4.5 — far less tolerant than dairy — and tom yum paste is built on citric acid. The 🥛 soy milk therefore goes in **off the heat, by tempering**, and nothing boils afterwards.
> **(2) Acid greys broccoli.** Chlorophyll in an acidic broth converts to pheophytin and the florets turn olive-drab within minutes. The 🥦 broccoli is steamed separately and meets the soup in the bowl.

1. **Cook the 🫘 chickpeas.** Rinse 250 g dried chickpeas — **no soaking needed** — into the pressure cooker with ~900 ml water. **No salt, and above all no acid**: acid keeps legume pectin from solubilising and the skins stay stubbornly tough, so the tom yum paste must not go anywhere near this step. **35 minutes at high pressure, natural release** (Nussinow: 30–40 min unsoaked). ⚠ **Natural release only** — the Ninja rule, and correct for legumes anyway, since a fast release blows the skins off and foams starchy water through the valve. Drain, **reserving 400 ml of the cooking liquid.**
2. **Prep while it runs.** Dice the 🧅 onion, mince the 🧄 garlic, cut the 🥕 carrots into 1.5 cm coins, break the 🥦 broccoli into florets and peel and slice the stem — the stem is the best part and most of its weight is usually thrown away.
3. **Steam the 🥦 broccoli** 4–5 minutes, until it's bright green and a floret stem still resists a knife tip. Pull it *early*; it carries on cooking. **Set aside.** With no air fryer in this kitchen, this is the only texture in the bowl — a soft floret makes the whole thing one-note.
4. **Crack the 🥥 cream, and fry the 🧅 onion in it.** Open the can **without shaking it** — Ayam's cream is 100% coconut kernel extract with no gums, so it separates, and the top of the can is nearly pure fat. Spoon ~40 g of that thick layer into the pan and heat until it stops looking like cream and the oil visibly breaks out of it. Fry the onion in that, ~6 minutes, until soft and just colouring; 🧄 garlic for the last 30 seconds. **No added oil is needed** — this is the classic Thai way to start a curry and it uses fat the dish already has.
5. **Fry the 🍲 paste hard**, 2–3 minutes, until it darkens, smells cooked rather than raw, and the fat visibly separates out of it. **Do not skip or shorten this** — a paste stirred straight into liquid tastes flat and raw, and this step is the single biggest difference between the tom yum cooks that scored well and the ones that didn't.
6. **Simmer the 🥕 carrots.** Add the carrots and the reserved 400 ml chickpea liquid, and simmer ~8 minutes. Add the drained 🫘 chickpeas and give it another 3–4 minutes so they take on the seasoning — they went in unsalted and this is where they get it.
7. **The rest of the 🥥 cream in.** Stir through and warm 2 minutes. ⚠ **Don't boil it.** With no gum or emulsifier in the can there is nothing holding this emulsion together but the coconut's own protein, so hard heat will break it into oil and solids.
8. **Take the pan off the heat, then temper the 🥛 soy milk.** Pour the litre of soy milk into a jug. Ladle the hot broth **into the jug**, two ladles at a time, whisking between. Then return the whole thing to the pan. **Direction matters and this is the whole trick:** what curdles soy is the *local* pH where it first meets the acid. Pour soy into an acidic pot and the first of it hits full-strength acid and seizes. Pour acidic broth into a large volume of soy and every addition is diluted and buffered on contact. Same acid, same soy, completely different result.
9. **Warm to serving temperature only.** Low heat, no simmer, no boil. If it looks thin, blend ~150 g of the cooked 🫘 chickpeas with a ladle of broth and stir it back — fat-free body, and smooth enough to stay off Lara's soft-lump radar.
10. **Cook the 🌾 buckwheat in the pot the chickpeas just left.** Rinse it out, then 200 g groats to 400 ml water, lid **off**, simmered ~10 minutes until the water is gone, then 5 minutes off the heat with the lid on to steam out. Do **not** pressure-cook it — at 2–3 minutes it would be done before the cooker finished the natural release the Ninja rule requires, and it would arrive as porridge. ⚠ **If yours is kasha (pre-toasted), don't toast it further** — see the design note; rinse and cook it straight.
11. **Serve in layers, not stirred together.** 🌾 Buckwheat in the bottom of the bowl, soup ladled over, steamed 🥦 broccoli on top. 🍋 Lime wedges and 🌶️ chilli at the table. **Keep the grain out of the soup pot** — stirred in, it goes on drinking broth and its starch keeps thickening, so tomorrow's leftovers would be porridge rather than soup.

## Design notes

- **Why the 🫘 chickpeas cook alone, in plain water.** Three separate reasons converge on the same answer. Acid keeps legume pectin from dissolving, so chickpeas cooked in a tom yum broth stay tough no matter how long they go. Milk of either kind scorches on a pressure cooker base and curdles under pressure. And the 35 minutes chickpeas need would reduce 🥕 carrots to pulp and 🥦 broccoli to sulphurous mush. Cooking them apart isn't a compromise — it's the only order that works.
- **No air fryer is a real loss, and 🥦 broccoli is the compensation.** The Tom Yum Butternut Curry's 8.60 leaned on crisp-roasted cauliflower against a soft base; that lever isn't available in this kitchen. Steamed-firm broccoli held out of the acid until serving is the substitute — which is why step 3 says pull it early and step 10 says add it in the bowl. Overcook it and the dish has no texture at all.
- **Two liquids doing two different jobs.** The 🥥 cream is 28.5% fat and brings the richness, the mouthfeel and — once cracked — the frying medium; the 🥛 NutriSoy is 4.3% protein and brings the volume, the protein and — settled, not hoped for — 2000 mg of calcium. Neither would carry the dish alone: a litre of coconut cream would be inedible and a litre and a quarter of soy would be thin and beany. Together they land at 3.9 g fat and 3.8 g protein per 100 g.
- **Cream rather than milk changes two things beyond the numbers.** Because Ayam's can is 100% coconut kernel extract with **no gum or emulsifier**, it separates on standing — which is what makes step 4 possible, and is the reason no avocado oil appears in this recipe at all. The same absence cuts the other way at step 7: nothing is holding that emulsion together but coconut protein, so it splits under a hard boil more readily than a stabilised light coconut milk would. The no-boil rule was already there for the soy; the cream now depends on it too.
- **Tempering direction is the whole soy defence.** The sweet potato soup solved this by putting the soy in at the blender, where the acid disperses instantly. There's no blender step here — this is a chunky soup — so the defence had to change. Tempering *into* the soy achieves the same thing by a different route: it keeps local acid concentration low at every moment of contact. Pouring soy into the pot would undo it.
- **The soya milk turned out to be the best thing in the dish nutritionally.** NutriSoy is 200 mg calcium per 100 ml — about double an ordinary fortified soya drink and five times an unfortified one — so the litre alone carries 2000 mg and the finished soup 2644 mg, against the bone-health flag. It is also **4.32 g protein per 100 ml**, higher than the UK M&S carton it replaces, which is why the dish clears 124 g of protein with no tofu in it at all. Two caveats in the other direction: **no B12** (the M&S carton had it), and the carton is **946 ml** rather than a round litre. Its **stabiliser** is unnamed on the label but works in the tempering step's favour; it does not make the no-boil rule optional.
- **⚠ 🌾 Buckwheat is a flavour risk, and the serving architecture is what manages it.** Buckwheat is dark, earthy and assertive; tom yum is bright, sour and lemongrass-forward. They are not natural partners, and **rice would be the obvious grain for this bowl**. Three things make buckwheat work anyway: it is cooked in **plain water, apart**, so it never seasons the broth; it sits **under** the soup rather than through it, so each spoonful is mostly soup with some grain rather than a homogenised mixture; and its earthiness has the coconut fat to land against. ⚠ **Toasted (kasha) is the risky form** — toasting is exactly what makes buckwheat taste most like itself. If there's a choice, use **untoasted groats**, which are far milder. If it's kasha, don't toast it further.
- **Why it is not cooked in the soup.** Buckwheat sheds a lot of starch. Simmered in the broth it would thicken the pot, keep thickening in the fridge, and go on absorbing liquid overnight — this is a 3.8 kg batch made explicitly for leftovers, and stirred-in grain is what turns day-two soup into porridge. Cooking it apart also lets the portion be set per person, which matters with a heat-sensitive eight-year-old and a narrow rater at the table.
- **What the grain actually adds beyond carbs.** 200 g dry brings 150 g of carbohydrate and 692 kcal — the point of the exercise — but also **442 mg of magnesium**, which takes the dish to 1246 mg, and 20 g of fibre. It also lifts protein to 147 g in a dish with no tofu or dairy in it. The cost is that the finished weight grows by ~600 g of unsalted bulk, which is why the salt density falls from 0.29 to 0.24 g/100 g.
- **⚠ Family flags.** **Lara** — no in-pot lime; the paste's own acid is the only acid, which is the same arrangement she scored 7.5 and 7.6 on across the two tom yum dishes, and observation 55 has her preferring broccoli to asparagus, so the green here is in her favour. A **split or curdled** soup is squarely her stated failure mode, which raises the stakes on step 8. **Jannes** — heat-sensitive, so no chilli in the pot; 45 g of paste across 3.2 kg is mild. ⚠ He dislikes cauliflower, and broccoli is close enough that this is a genuine risk rather than a formality. **Anja** rewards savoury and textured over sweet and smooth — the chickpeas and the broccoli bite are both for her.
- **⚠ What's missing versus the good tom yum cooks.** No 🍄 mushroom powder and no 🍃 makrut lime leaves. Both are unconfirmed in the Singapore kitchen (`docs/PANTRY.md`). **Mushroom powder is the bigger loss** — it supplies guanylate, which multiplies against the paste's glutamate, and it's the project's standard way to fix an under-seasoned dish without adding salt. If there's any in a box, 6–8 g whisked in off heat at step 9 is the single largest upgrade available here.
- **⚠ Pantry assumptions.** This is written against the **HollyFarms** jar because that's the tom yum paste confirmed in Singapore. 🧅 onion, 🧄 garlic and 🥑 oil are all unconfirmed. The oil no longer matters — the recipe was rewritten around the coconut cream doing that job, so there is nothing to substitute.
