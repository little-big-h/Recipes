# Yellow Curry Corn Soup with Chickpeas, Broccoli & Shiitake

*Cooked with Jannes. A creamy yellow-curry corn soup built to solve one specific problem: **broccoli that stays green**. Every tom yum version of this dish forces the broccoli out of the pot, because acid turns chlorophyll to pheophytin and the florets go olive-drab within minutes. 🍛 Thai yellow curry paste carries no meaningful acid, so the 🥦 broccoli can cook in the soup and come out coated — the presentation that has consistently worked for Jannes with brassicas — and 🌿 lemongrass paste supplies the citrus lift that tom yum would otherwise have brought. The 🍄 shiitake is blended **completely**: the kids reject visible mushroom, and full blending extracts all of the guanylate anyway, so there is no trade-off. The 🫘 chickpeas are leftovers and only need heating through. 🌾 Millet is toasted and cooked in its own pan on the stove, so the soup can be reheated as soup rather than setting into a block. Family of five.*

## Method

> **Prep:** Dice the 🧅 onion, mince the 🧄 garlic. Weigh the 🥦 frozen broccoli into two lots — the smaller one goes in early and is blended into the base, the larger goes in whole at the end — and leave both in the freezer until they are called for. **If you are using dried shiitake instead of frozen, tear the stems off before soaking** — dried shiitake stems are woody, a domestic blender will not break them down, and shredded stem fibre is exactly the texture the kids will find.
>
> ⚠ **Frozen broccoli, not fresh.** There are no usable stems in a frozen bag, so the sliced-stem share that would have gone into the base is taken from the florets instead. It is also already blanched, so it needs **2–3 minutes, not four**, and it will not hold a bite the way fresh does — ice crystals rupture the cell walls, so it cooks softer however carefully it is timed. The bitterness survives; the texture contrast does not, which is why none of the 🫘 chickpeas get blended.

1. **Toast and cook the 🌾 millet, in its own pan.** Toast the dry grains over medium heat, stirring, 2–3 minutes until they smell nutty. Add 500 ml water and a pinch of salt, bring to a boil, cover, and simmer on low **25 minutes**. Pull it off the heat and leave it covered to steam until serving. Toasting is the single biggest quality difference with millet — it keeps the grains distinct instead of pasty.

2. **Fry the 🧅 onion.** Heat the 🥑 avocado oil in the soup pot and cook the onion about 6 minutes, until soft and just colouring. 🧄 Garlic for the last 30 seconds.

3. **Bloom the 🍛 paste — gently, and stop early.** Stir the 🍛 yellow curry paste and 🌿 lemongrass paste into the onions off the fiercest heat, **60–90 seconds on medium**, until it smells cooked and the oil takes colour. ⚠ Do **not** fry it hard until it darkens — that cue scorches a sweetened paste. This jar is only 2.9 g sugars per 100 g so it is far more forgiving than the Hollyfarms tom yum, but the window is still narrower than it feels. **Wet it immediately** with a splash of the 💧 water the moment it smells right.

4. **🍄 Shiitake, in the microwave, in a bowl of its own — start this while the onions are frying.** Put the frozen shiitake in a bowl with about 150 ml of the 💧 water per 200 g of mushroom, cover loosely, and microwave in bursts — 2 minutes, stir, then 30–60 second increments — until it is **hot but still, with only faint steam and no bubbling**. That is roughly **65 °C**. Then **leave it to stand 15 minutes** while you get on with the soup. Tip the whole bowl in at step 5, liquid included; that liquid is the best thing in the dish.

   ⚠ **Two enzymes, pulling in opposite directions, and this is why the temperature is worth steering.** Ribonuclease converts the mushroom's RNA into 5′-GMP — the guanylate that multiplies against corn's glutamate — and works best at **60–70 °C**. Phosphomonoesterase *destroys* 5′-GMP into flavourless guanosine, and works best at **40–50 °C**. So the aim is to move **quickly through the low band**, **hold in the high one** (the standing time is where most of the guanylate is actually made), and only then go hot enough to denature both. A bowl can be steered to 65 °C; a six-kilo pot cannot, which is the whole reason this step leaves the pot. **Err cool** — undershooting costs a little, overshooting denatures the ribonuclease before it has worked and costs all of it. Never microwave it dry: it scorches at the edges and steams in the middle.

5. **💧 Water, 🌽 corn and the first 🥦 broccoli in.** Add the rest of the water, the frozen sweetcorn, the bowl of 🍄 shiitake and its liquid, and **150 g of the frozen broccoli** — the share that will be blended into the base. Simmer about 10 minutes. The pot can start hot; nothing here needs coaxing now that the shiitake is made.

6. **Blend — 🍄 shiitake first, and thoroughly.** Transfer most of the pot to the blender and run it **60–90 seconds**. Shiitake cell walls are chitinous and tougher than plant fibre; stopping at "looks smooth" leaves the faint fur that gets noticed. ⚠ **Get the shiitake and corn smooth before any 🌾 millet comes near the blender** — cooked millet under that much shear releases amylose and turns gluey. The millet stays in its own pan and never goes in.

7. **Back to the pot, then 🫘 chickpeas and 🥥 coconut milk.** Return the purée, stir in the already-cooked chickpeas and the coconut milk, and warm 4 minutes. The chickpeas went in unsalted, so this is where they take on the seasoning.

8. **The rest of the 🥦 broccoli in, straight from frozen, for 2–3 minutes.** It is already blanched, so this is the easiest step in the recipe to overshoot — it only needs heating through and a moment more. ⚠ Longer and brassica glucosinolates break down into sulphur compounds; you want the bitterness, not the cabbage.

9. **Finish off the heat.** Stir in the 🌰 shiitake powder if you have it — no special timing, the guanylate in a dried powder is already formed and its enzymes are long dead. Then **🧂 salt, and taste properly.** See the design notes: the paste alone leaves this pot well below the salt level of the dishes that have scored well here.

10. **Serve** with the 🌾 millet spooned into each bowl and the soup ladled over, and 🍋 lime wedges at the table.

## Design notes

**⚠ Salt is the thing to get right, and the paste will not do it alone — which is why 8 g is planned in.** At 110 g of The SOS Kitchen yellow paste and nothing else, the whole pot would carry **3.2 g of salt across ~4.7 kg — 0.068 g per 100 g**. The dishes that have scored well in this kitchen run at **0.28–0.30 g per 100 g** (the tom yum broccoli/chickpea soup landed at 0.28), and the family pot's usual band is 10–15 g of salt total. The 8 g at step 9 brings this pot to **10.9 g, or 0.23 g per 100 g** — just inside the band, and deliberately at its lower edge so there is room to taste upward rather than down.

This is very likely the whole explanation for "the paste tastes a little bland." The SOS jar is **824 mg sodium per 100 g against Hollyfarms tom yum's 6267** — about a seventh. Paste quantities carried over from the tom yum jar therefore under-salt by roughly that factor, and no amount of blooming technique fixes a dish that is seven times under-seasoned. Dose the paste high *and* salt at the end; they are separate levers.

**Why yellow and not tom yum.** Two constraints from `tom-yum-broccoli-carrot-chickpea-soup.md` decide it. Acid turns broccoli olive-drab, which forces the florets out of the pot and into the bowl; and acid curdles soy, which forces a tempering step. Yellow curry paste carries no meaningful acid, so both problems disappear and the broccoli can cook *in* the soup — which matters, because coated-rather-than-visible is the brassica presentation that works for Jannes. ⚠ If your jar of SOS yellow tastes noticeably sour, it contains tamarind and both constraints come back; add the florets at the bowl instead.

**Why the shiitake is fully blended rather than part-blended.** The kids reject visible mushroom, so this was not really a choice — but it is also the better option. Nothing stays locked inside a slice, so full blending extracts all of the guanylate. The only cost is colour, and at 200 g of pale frozen shiitake in a 4.7 kg pot of yellow corn it is a shade, not a problem. Dried shiitake would be a different story: darker, and the woody stems do not blend.

**Why frozen shiitake rather than dried.** Frozen wins on three axes — it purées cleanly, it is pale, and it needs no soak, which matters with a child in the kitchen. Dried wins decisively on one: drying holds the mushroom for hours at enzyme-friendly temperatures and produces far more guanylate than freezing does. The resolution is to take both — frozen for the body, 🌰 shiitake powder for the punch, which is dried shiitake with the texture problem already removed.

**Why the 🍄 shiitake is made in a microwave and not in the pot.** An earlier version of step 4 had the frozen shiitake going into a cool pot and coming up slowly, on the reasoning that this kept it inside the 60–70 °C band where ribonuclease builds guanylate. That was half right and it is the step this recipe has since corrected. Slow warming also **dwells** at 40–50 °C, which is the optimum of phosphomonoesterase — the enzyme that destroys 5′-GMP. The temperature shape you actually want is fast through the low band, held in the high one, then hot. A bowl in a microwave can be steered that way; a six-kilo pot cannot be steered at all. Moving the step out of the pot also removes the constraint that the soup had to start cold, which matters when the pot is already near its volume limit and the corn wants to get going.

**Why not nutritional yeast.** It is a **glutamate** source, and so is corn; stacking them is additive. Shiitake is a **guanylate** source, and glutamate × guanylate is multiplicative. In a corn soup the glutamate is what you already have. The same argument rules out a kombu-blend seasoning powder here — kombu is very dense glutamate — though that blend is the better jar for a dish with no glutamate source of its own.

**Millet stays in its own pan.** Cooked in the soup it would thicken it beautifully and then keep thickening: millet is ~70% starch and the grains go on swelling, so a soup that is right in the pot is a solid block the next morning. Separate pan, spooned into bowls, and the leftovers reheat as soup.
