# Torn Seitan for Soup [09-11-26] ✴️

*Seitan built to be **torn, not sliced** — ragged strands that hold their shape in broth and grab it along the grain. 🫧 Yeast is still in it, but its job has changed: it is here to make **elongated voids along the fibres**, which are the planes of weakness the strands tear along. The gluten is worked by **stretching, never kneading into a ball**, and the log is twisted before it is wrapped so the grain runs the length of it.*

> ⚠⚠ **This replaces the "Yeast-Risen Besan Seitan" of the same date, which was built to be fluffy and sliced.** Tearing and fluffiness pull in opposite directions, and the soup settles it — see **Why this is not the fluffy version** under Design notes. The earlier file was never cooked, so nothing is preserved for comparison.

> ⚠⚠ **This is also the opposite recipe to [`pressure-steamed-seitan-sausage.md`](./pressure-steamed-seitan-sausage.md).** That one wants a dense, uniform, sliceable sausage and kneads into a tight ball. This one wants a directional grain and stretches instead. Both develop the gluten hard — **the difference is the direction**, and it is the whole recipe.

> ⚠ **COOKED 09-11-2026, and every quantity above is what actually went in.** The build held to the plan exactly, with **one deviation: 🥑 oil 24 g against 20 g** — the nutrition below carries it. **The outstanding items are all outcomes rather than quantities**: the yield out of the steamer, whether 90 minutes was enough, and whether it tears. See **Weigh-ins** under Design notes.

## Method

### 1. Besan — the pan, first

1. **Dry-roast the 🫘 besan**, no oil, 4–5 minutes over medium heat, stirring, until it smells nutty and darkens slightly. Light, not dark.
2. ⚠⚠ **Let it cool to room temperature before it goes near the yeast.** It comes off the pan well over 100 °C and will kill the 🫧 yeast outright. The easiest way to waste the whole batch.

### 2. Dough — mixed short, then stretched

3. **Combine all the dry**: 🌾 gluten, the cooled 🫘 besan, 🫓 atta, 🧀 nooch, 🫧 instant yeast, 🍬 sugar and 🧂 salt. Mix dry and thoroughly so the yeast is distributed before water arrives.
4. **Add the wet** — ~310 g warm 💧 water, 🥑 oil, 🦪 oyster sauce and 🍥 dashi powder — and mix **2–3 minutes**, to a cohesive, slightly firm dough. Longer than the fluffy version, and still not a kneading.
   > ⚠ **Do not knead it into a tight ball.** Kneading develops gluten in every direction at once, which is what makes a uniform, sliceable sausage. Here the gluten has to be developed in **one** direction.
5. **Rest 20 minutes, covered.** Relaxation is what lets the next step stretch far instead of snapping back.

### 3. Stretching — this is the recipe

6. **Three stretch cycles, ten minutes apart.** Each one: take the dough in both hands, **pull it out into a long rope** — as long as it will go without tearing — then **fold it back on itself in thirds** and set it down. Cover and rest ten minutes. Repeat.
   > **Each pull aligns more of the gluten along the length of the rope**, and each rest lets it relax enough for the next pull to go further than the last. This is the same mechanism as hand-pulled noodles, and it is the only step that decides whether the finished log tears into strands or breaks into lumps.
   > ⚠ **Stop pulling the moment it resists hard.** A torn dough has lost the continuity the fibres need; let it rest longer rather than forcing it.
7. **Rest 10 minutes after the third stretch**, then **pull it out one last time and twist the rope along its length**, four or five turns, like wringing a cloth. Coil the twisted rope loosely into a log.
   > **The twist is what puts the grain into a spiral**, so the finished piece tears along a curve rather than splitting into flat slabs.

> ✅ **The 310 g of water was right as specified, confirmed on the 09-11 cook** — it was measured rather than judged by feel and the dough came together without adjustment. Worth recording, because the project's other high-gluten dough (the Masala-Zwiebelkuchen) carries an explicit "judge the water by feel" instruction. At 0.86 : 1 against the dry weight this one can be measured.

### 4. Wrap and proof

8. **Wrap in foil, firmly but not tight** — the sausage recipe's sweet-wrapper twist is too tight here; the 🫧 yeast needs somewhere to go. Leave roughly a third again of slack.
9. **Proof 60 minutes, in the bamboo basket.** Singapore ambient is the right temperature. Proofing in the basket means the log is never lifted between the proof and the heat.
   > ⚠ **Shorter than a bread proof, deliberately.** The gas is wanted for the voids, not for volume, and **an over-proofed dough slackens and loses the fibre tension** the stretching just built. Sixty minutes, not ninety.

### 5. Steam — bamboo basket over a wok

10. **Bamboo steamer, 90 minutes over briskly boiling water**, the wrapped log sitting in the basket it proofed in.
    > ✅ **The bamboo basket is the better vessel here, not merely an acceptable one.** It gives **true saturated steam at 100 °C**, where the oven bain-marie was an approximation of it — and that heat is **gentler and more even than a 175 °C oven**, which matters because the log has to finish expanding before its surface sets. Gluten denatures at roughly 60–70 °C and the window is short.
    > ✅ **Bamboo absorbs condensate** rather than raining it back down, which is the one thing a metal steamer cannot do. With the log wrapped it changes little; it is the reason to reach for bamboo rather than a metal tier if both are out.
    > ✅ **Proof the log in the basket.** It then goes onto the wok in one movement, with no lifting or transfer to knock the gas out of a dough that has just spent an hour building it.
    > ⚠ **90 minutes, not 75.** The oven's hot walls radiate and drive heat in faster; saturated steam at 100 °C is gentler and therefore slower. ⚠ **This figure is an estimate with no measurement behind it** — see **Weigh-ins**.
    > ⚠⚠ **Top the wok up with BOILING water, never cold.** Ninety minutes will run a wok dry, and a dry wok under a bamboo basket scorches it and can crack it. Cold water from the tap drops the pot off the boil and stalls the cook for several minutes.
    > ⚠⚠ **Not the Instant Pot.** Its Steam program pressurises, and pressure compacts the grain back into the uniform block this method exists to avoid. ⚠ **Never submerge or simmer** — water logs the fibres and they go to mush.
    > **Alternative if the wok is wanted elsewhere:** 175 °C for **75 minutes** in a covered bain-marie — the wrapped log in a tray holding ~2 cm of hot water, the tray covered. It works; it is simply the second-best version of the same idea.

### 6. Cool, then tear

11. ⚠⚠ **Cool completely in the wrap, ideally overnight in the fridge. This step is not optional here, it is load-bearing.** Warm seitan has not finished setting and will smear rather than tear; cold seitan separates cleanly along the grain. **Overnight is the difference between strands and lumps.**
12. **Tear by hand, along the grain. Do not cut.** A knife goes across the fibres and gives you cubes; hands follow them and give you strands.
13. ⚠ **Brown the torn pieces before they meet the broth.** Dry pan or a little oil, hard heat, until the ragged edges catch. Torn surfaces have an enormous area — searing them builds Maillard flavour *and* a skin that resists waterlogging. Skipping this is the main reason seitan goes spongy in soup.

### 7. Into the soup — late

14. **Add in the last five minutes, or put the pieces in the bowl and ladle the broth over them.**
    > ⚠ **Do not simmer it in the soup.** It is already fully cooked, it adds nothing to the broth, and prolonged simmering is exactly what turns a good strand into a waterlogged sponge. The pieces should meet hot liquid, not cook in it.

## Design notes

- **Why this is not the fluffy version.** A fine, even crumb **crumbles**; it does not tear. Tearing needs **anisotropy** — gluten aligned along one axis, with weak planes between the strands — and the fluffy formula was built to destroy exactly that, by diluting the network with 🫘 besan and 🥔 potato starch and by never working it directionally. **The soup settles the argument independently:** a light, bread-like seitan dropped in broth becomes a waterlogged dumpling within minutes, while a fibrous one holds its shape and takes the broth up along the grain. Two different briefs pointed the same way, so the formula moved.
- **🫧 The yeast stays, and it is doing a different job now.** Not lift — **voids**. Gas cells that form in a stretched, aligned dough are **elongated along the fibres**, and those elongated voids are the planes of weakness the strands separate along. Yeast plus alignment is what gives shreddable seitan; alignment alone gives something closer to rubber. This is why the proof is kept to 60 minutes: enough gas to open the grain, not so much that the dough slackens and loses the tension.
- **🫓 Atta is still here for α-amylase and still cannot be left out.** Yeast ferments maltose and simple sugars; it has **no amylase and cannot eat starch.** Vital wheat gluten is washed dough, so the starch and every water-soluble enzyme are gone and the residual carbohydrate on the label has nothing left to convert it. The 🫓 atta brings the enzyme; the 🍬 sugar covers the first half hour before the enzyme produces anything.
- **🫘 Besan halved rather than dropped.** It interrupts the gluten network, which is now a cost rather than a benefit — but 30 g still earns its place on flavour and on the fibre and folate it carries, and at that dose it does not break up the grain.
- **🥔 Potato starch removed entirely.** Its only job was to stay soft and tender, and tenderness is not the target any more. It adds no protein structure and actively fights fibre formation.
- **💧 Hydration down from ~1 : 1 to about 0.86 : 1.** A slack dough cannot hold an alignment — it flows back. A firmer dough keeps the shape of each pull, which is what makes three stretches cumulative rather than three attempts at the same thing.
- **🧂 Salt helps here.** It tightens the gluten network, which was the wrong direction for a fluffy loaf and is the right one for a dough that has to be stretched without tearing.
- **Considered and not used:** laminating the dough with oil between layers (gives sheets rather than strands — the right technique for a mock duck, the wrong one for soup pieces); boiling in broth, which is the traditional seitan method and the single fastest way to waterlog it; and raising the gluten to 100% with no flour at all, which would give the strongest fibres and no rise whatever, since nothing would feed the yeast.

### Weigh-ins for the first cook

| Measurement | Why |
|:------------|:----|
| **Dough into the wrap → log out of the steam** | ⚠ **Still outstanding.** The ~700 g yield is a guess and no wrapped-steam loss figure exists anywhere on file. With 753 g going in, this is one weighing. |
| **Was 90 minutes in the bamboo enough?** | The steaming time is an estimate derived from the oven figure, not a measurement. Probe the centre, or cut the first log in half and look — undercooked seitan is visibly wet and pasty in the middle, and it does not improve on cooling. |
| **Rope length at the first stretch → at the third** | The direct measure of whether the rests are doing their work. If the third pull is no longer than the first, the rests are too short. |
| **Does it tear, or does it break?** | The go/no-go, and not a number. Breaking into lumps means either not enough stretching or not enough cooling — test the cooling first, it is free. |
| **Torn warm vs torn cold, from the same log** | Tear a piece while it is still warm and another the next morning. One cheap comparison that settles how load-bearing the overnight rest really is. |
| **Browned vs un-browned in the bowl** | Half the pieces seared, half not, same soup. Tests whether the browning step is worth the pan it dirties. |
