## Method

### 1. Shiitake — big wok, ahead of time

1. **🍄‍🟫 600 g of shiitake caps, dry in the big wok**, over high heat. Water out, water off, *then* 🥑 8 g oil, then colour.
   > ⚠⚠ **The big wok, not the small one.** 600 g in the small stainless wok is three layers deep: the water cannot leave, and the mushrooms steam grey instead of browning. The small wok tops out around 200 g of shiitake per batch.
   > ⚠ **Oil only once the surfaces have gone matte.** Oil added to wet mushrooms makes a barrier that traps the water it is supposed to help drive off.
   > **This holds.** Brown it hours ahead if that suits the evening better.

### 2. Roots — two trays, two different dice

2. **🍠 purple sweet potato at 15 mm on ceramic tray B, 🎃 butternut at 25 mm on ceramic tray A**, ~1 g 🥑 oil per 100 g, **220 °C convection with fan, ~30 min**.
   > ⚠ **As cooked 10-11: 830 g of 🍠 sweet potato with 3 g of oil and 546 g of 🎃 squash with 2 g — 0.36 and 0.37 g per 100 g**, against the ~1 g per 100 g the losses table's roasting row was measured at. Expect less browning, and treat the resulting loss figure as **not strictly comparable** to the 16.4% and 15.8% already on file.
   > ⚠ **830 g of 15 mm dice is a lot for one tray.** Small dice pack densely; if any of it sits two deep it steams rather than roasts, which costs both the browning and a clean measurement. Spread it or split it.
   > **Preheat the ceramic hard.** Ceramic conducts far more slowly than steel, so a cold tray costs the roots their browned underside; given a long preheat it holds that heat well once it has it. Tares are **A 654 g, B 584 g** — weigh each tray loaded, before and after, and both vegetables give their own loss figure.
   > ⚠⚠ **The different dice is the whole point, not fussiness.** Purple sweet potato is denser and drier; butternut is softer and carries more free sugar, so its edges catch first. At equal dice the butternut is done while the sweet potato is still firm. Cut the sweet potato smaller and they finish together.
   > ⚠ **One tray each, not both on one.** A 40 × 30 cm tray holds about 700–800 g of dice in a single layer. Crowd 1 kg onto it and they steam in their own moisture — pale, soft cubes instead of browned edges. That failure is far likelier than any problem with mixing the two vegetables.
   > On file: roasted sweet potato loses **16.4%**, butternut **15.8%** — near enough identical, which is why they suit the same oven and the same oil rate.

### 3. Buckwheat — Instant Pot

3. **Steep the 🍄‍🟫 frozen shiitake stems first, in the water the 🌰 buckwheat will absorb.** 600 g water into the inner pot, lid on and **locked**, **Sous Vide → Chicken, 60 °C**. When it reaches temperature, **then** drop the frozen stems in. Hold **30 minutes**, stirring once midway.
   > ⚠⚠ **The stems go in AFTER the bath is at temperature, never at the start, and this is the whole reason for using sous vide rather than the pressure cycle.** Phosphomonoesterase **destroys** 5′-GMP at **40–50 °C**; ribonuclease **makes** it at **60–70 °C**. Freezing ruptures the cells but **does not kill the enzymes**, so stems sitting in water that climbs slowly from cold spend the climb in the destructive band. A pressure cycle does exactly that and then overshoots to ~110 °C. See `../../docs/TECHNIQUES.md`.
   > **Chicken at 60 °C, not Egg at 70 °C.** 60 °C is inside the productive band with margin; 70 °C is its top edge and risks denaturing the enzyme you are relying on.
   > ⚠ **No circulation pump and the element is in the base**, so the bath stratifies and 600 g is a shallow layer. Stir at the start and once midway.
   > ⚠ **An absorption cook keeps everything you infuse**, which is the point — nothing is drained away. But **take the stems out before the grain goes in.** They stay woody through any cook; that is why they were bagged for dashi in the first place.
   > ⚠ **Weigh the strained liquid and correct it back to 600 g.** It can go either way: frozen stems have ruptured cells and give water up at 60 °C, so the volume may rise rather than fall. A sealed absorption cook cannot be topped up mid-cycle, and dry is the direction that leaves hard grains.
4. ⚠ **AS COOKED 10-11: 247 g of kasha, not 400 g — so 370 g of the steeped liquor, not 600 g.** That leaves **~230 g of shiitake liquor surplus. It goes into the sauce**, in place of part of the 🥛 soya milk: it is the same infusion the grain is getting, and the light coconut milk needs loosening anyway. Do not pour it away.

4. *(As designed)* **🌰 400 g kasha into the strained 600 g — 1.5 : 1, not the usual 1.75 : 1.** Absorption cook: **0 minutes at low pressure**, then **wait ten minutes by the clock and vent by hand.**
   > ⚠ **Deliberately drier than the measured row.** Buckwheat has no gluten and a soft starchy endosperm, and it keeps absorbing after it is cooked — under a sauce it slumps. The **2.53×** yield in `../../docs/TECHNIQUES.md` was measured at 1.75 : 1; at 1.5 : 1 expect about **2.3×**, so ~920 g.
   > ⚠ **There is no automatic timed release on the Instant Pot.** "Ten-minute defined release" means exactly that: set a timer when the cycle ends, then vent manually. That is what was done when the 2.53× figure was taken.
   > ⚠ **If raw green groats rather than pre-toasted kasha**, dry-toast them first. That is the difference between separate grains and porridge.

### 4. The sauce — the linchpin

5. ⚠⚠ **AS COOKED 10-11: 200 ml of Kara LIGHT UHT coconut milk, not the Ayam cream — and that breaks the step below.** A light UHT milk is **homogenised and stabilised**, so it does **not** separate and there is **no fat cap to fry the paste in**. **Fry the paste in 🥑 10 g of oil instead.** ✅ The compensation: the Ayam’s no-gum purity was the whole reason for the never-boil-hard rule, and a stabilised milk holds together far better — but it will not reduce to richness, so do not try.

5. *(As designed, with the Ayam cream)* **Open the 🥥 coconut cream and do not shake the can.** It separates; **spoon off the thick top and fry the paste in that.** No added oil is needed — the cream is 28.5% fat.
6. ⚠⚠ **AS COOKED 10-11: NO 🧅 onion in the paste** — so it is 135 g of 🌾 lemongrass, 🧄 garlic, 🫚 ginger and 🌱 coriander root rather than 215 g. The onion was over a third of it and the wet, forgiving third. **What is left is concentrated and low-moisture, and garlic burns bitter: lower the heat, keep it moving, and expect 2–3 minutes rather than 4–5.** It also removes bulk and sweetness, so **loosen with the surplus shiitake liquor rather than the 🥛 soya milk** — you may not need the soya milk at all.

6. *(As designed)* **Pound to a paste**: 🌾 lemongrass, 🫚 ginger, 🧄 garlic, 🧅 onion and the 🌱 coriander **stems and roots**.
   > ⚠⚠ **🌾 Lemongrass: the tender lower 10 cm only, and it must be pounded or sliced to nothing.** It is intensely fibrous — any piece big enough to notice is a piece nobody can chew. The upper stalks go to the freezer; 350 g is months of supply.
   > **🌱 Coriander roots and stems carry most of the flavour** and they take heat; the leaves do not. Leaves go on at the end.
7. **Fry the paste in the coconut fat** until it smells cooked and the fat separates again, 4–5 minutes.
8. **🍃 Eight whole makrut leaves in, bruised**, then the rest of the 🥥 cream, the 🥛 soya milk, 🦪 oyster sauce, 🍥 dashi powder, 🧀 nooch and 🧂 salt. **Simmer gently 8 minutes, then fish the leaves out.**
   > ⚠⚠ **Do not let it boil hard.** The Ayam cream is 100% coconut kernel extract with **no gums or emulsifiers**, which is why it tastes clean and also why it **splits more readily than a stabilised brand**.
   > **🍃 Whole in, then out — then four more shredded fine, stirred in off the heat.** The infused leaves give the deep background; the fresh shred gives the top note that cooking destroys. The central rib is tough, so strip it before shredding.
9. **Taste for body.** It should coat a spoon and run off it, not cling. ⚠⚠ **With the light milk the 🥛 soya milk drops from 250 g to about 80 g** — the 250 g existed to loosen a 28.5% cream, and against 200 ml of light milk it would give coloured water. Add it a spoon at a time and stop when it coats.

### 5. Leaves, then assemble

10. ⚠⚠ **Stems and leaves are two ingredients, not one, and they do not go in together.** **Thin stems first**, cut 2–3 cm, **60–90 seconds over heat**; **then the 🌿 leaves, off the heat**, folded through until just collapsed. Treat them the same and you get either woody stems or dead leaves.
    > **Keep the thin stems — they are the crunch.** Same mechanism as the puay leng stems in the shakshuka: it is **turgor, not fibre**, and sustained submersion is what destroys it. They are also the more mucilaginous part, which a coconut sauce carries and a thin broth would not. ⚠ **Snap test:** a stem that breaks cleanly is tender; one that bends and pulls a string is woody — discard or peel those. Expect the base of each stem to fail it.
    > ⚠ **Keeping the stems changes the yield, though less than first claimed.** As trimmed on 10-11 the stems came to **77 g — 22% of the 350 g bunch**, against an assumption that they were a larger share. Usable material goes from roughly 200 g to nearer **270 g**, not the 300 g first estimated. ⚠ **USDA  is a LEAF record**; stems are higher in water and lower in nutrient density, so costing 77 g of stem against it overstates them slightly.
   > ⚠ **350 g will read as roughly 80 g.** They are also mildly mucilaginous — a coconut sauce carries that well, a thin broth would not — and they go slippery if they sit in a hot pan. In, folded, out.
11. **Bowls: 🌰 buckwheat, then the roots and 🍄‍🟫 shiitake, then 🫛 edamame, sauce over, 🫑 raw baby peppers and 🌱 coriander leaves on top.** 🍋‍🟩 Lime wedges on the table.
    > 🫑 **The peppers are raw and stay raw.** Lara is fine with raw pepper and flagged against cooked — this costs nothing and removes a whole objection.
    > ⚪ **Acid at the table, not in the pot.** Observation 60.

## Design notes

- ⚠ **All three umami levers went up together on the day** — 🧀 nooch 10 → 16 g, 🦪 oyster sauce 25 → 39 g, 🍥 dashi powder 5 → 16 g. Individually each is small; together the sauce is markedly more savoury than designed. That is the right direction against two roasted roots and coconut, but it is a large combined move — **taste the sauce before adding anything else to it.**
- **The sauce is the only thing that has to be fresh.** Shiitake, roots and buckwheat all hold; a bowl dish is a set of components plus one binding element, and the binding element is where the attention goes. That is also why the sauce carries all three umami sources — 🧀 glutamate, and guanylate from both the 🍥 dashi and the mushroom-based 🦪 LKK sauce — rather than spreading them across the components.
- **This bowl has the corn soup's sweetness problem and the same fix.** Two roasted roots plus coconut cream is sweet by default, exactly as 1.5 kg of corn was. The counters are identical: **savoury depth built in** (the three umami sources, the hard-browned 🍄‍🟫 shiitake) and **acid at the table** (🍋‍🟩 lime). The Cantonese soup scored 8.53 on that combination the day before.
- **Two roots, two dice, two trays.** Three separate reasons, and only the first is about the vegetables: density differs, sugar content differs, and a single tray cannot hold a kilo in one layer.
- **🌿 Sweet potato leaves are the reason this is a Tuesday dish.** At 2–3 days they are the most perishable thing in the box, and spending them on day one is correct whichever meal they go into.
- **Lara, in one line:** 🫑 raw peppers clear her flag, there is no acid in the pot, and the 🍄‍🟫 mushroom is visible, which is her objection from the Zwiebelkuchen (obs 58). The lever if wanted is blending her share of the mushroom into the sauce rather than scattering it on top. Not specced, because it has not been asked for.
- **Considered and not used:** 🫘 tau kwa as the protein (it browns and dices well, and the ~12% seasoned-browning loss is on file) — left open rather than specced, since 🫛 edamame and the leftover seitan both need no preparation; and galangal in the paste, which is not confirmed in the kitchen.
